ERP master data and material flow

The shortages looked like a supplier problem. They were a master data problem — and master data is engineering work, not clerical work.

75% Reduction in material shortages
15% Reduction in order scheduling errors
Infor LN MBOMs · routings · capacity

The situation

Builds were stalling for want of parts. The visible symptom was material shortages at the line, and the instinctive explanation was supplier performance — a story that is easy to tell and almost never the whole cause.

An ERP plans against what it has been told. If a lead time in the item master is wrong, MRP will faithfully place the order too late, every single time, and the shortage arrives on schedule. Nothing in the system flags this, because from the ERP's point of view it did exactly what it was configured to do.

A shortage at the line is a symptom. The question is whether the part was ordered late, or ordered on time and staged in the wrong place.

What I did

1. Treat master data as engineering data

I took ownership of ERP master data in Infor LN — MBOMs, routings, and capacity — as engineering deliverables rather than as administrative records. Manufacturing BOMs, routing times, and lead times drive every downstream plan: purchase timing, capacity loading, scheduling, and cost. When they drift from reality, every plan built on them is wrong in the same direction at once.

2. Correct lead times

I optimized lead time setup so that planned order release reflected actual replenishment. This is unglamorous and high-leverage: a lead time that is too short guarantees recurring shortages, while one that is too long inflates inventory and hides the real constraint. Neither error announces itself — both look like ordinary operational noise until someone checks the parameter against reality.

3. Fix line-side inventory setup

The second half was line-side inventory configuration — governing which parts are staged at the point of use versus pulled from stores. Getting this wrong produces a distinctive and confusing failure: the part is in the building but not at the station, so the line stops while inventory records show healthy stock. Every hour spent looking for a part that was never missing is pure non-value-added time.

4. Close the information gaps between functions

Material flow problems live in the gaps between departments, so I worked the problem across sales, purchasing, planning, quality, and operations — the full chain from order intake to the station. Each function had part of the picture and none had all of it; the shortages were largely produced by the handoffs rather than by any one group's decisions.

Closing those gaps streamlined material flow, reduced WIP, and improved value stream balance — WIP being the most honest indicator of whether material and production are actually synchronized.

Results

MeasureOutcomeMechanism
Material shortages Down 75% Lead time and line-side inventory setup in Infor LN

Scope Multiple builds Sustained across the product range, not a single order

A 75% reduction in shortages without renegotiating a single supplier contract is the clearest evidence available that the constraint was internal. The parts were largely obtainable the whole time; the system was asking for them at the wrong moment, or putting them in the wrong place.

What generalizes

  • Check the parameters before blaming the suppliers. An incorrect lead time produces a perfectly regular shortage that looks exactly like supplier failure.
  • Master data is engineering work. MBOMs, routings, and lead times are process definitions. Treated as clerical records, they drift, and every plan drifts with them.
  • "In stock" and "at the station" are different questions. Line-side setup decides which one you get.
  • Material problems live in the handoffs. The fix usually requires the whole chain in the room, not a better performing individual function.
  • WIP tells the truth. It is the fastest read on whether material and production are actually in sync.
