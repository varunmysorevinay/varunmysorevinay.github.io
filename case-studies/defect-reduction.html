Harsco Rail · Manufacturing Engineer

Defect reduction through DMAIC

Structured root cause analysis applied to recurring defects rather than to their symptoms — and closed out in the design and process documents so they stayed fixed.

20% Defect reduction
13% Reduction in rework and scrap
A3 · 5-Why · 8D Applied within DMAIC

The situation

Defects in heavy equipment assembly are expensive in a particular way. A problem found at final test has already absorbed the full build cost, and correcting it means disassembly, rework, re-inspection, and schedule loss. The cost is nothing like the price of the part.

The failure mode that matters is not the individual defect — it is the recurring defect. A defect that comes back has, by definition, been "fixed" before without its cause being removed. That is a containment loop: real work, real cost, no permanent change.

A defect that recurs was never solved. It was contained, and containment is a cost you pay again every time.

What I did

1. DMAIC as the frame

I drove defect reduction through DMAIC — Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control. The value of the frame is mostly in the bookends. Measure forces agreement on the actual defect rate before anyone proposes a fix, and Control forces the question of what will hold the gain after attention moves elsewhere. Most quality efforts that fail have skipped one or both.

2. Match the tool to the problem

Within that frame I used A3, 5-Why, and 8D — not interchangeably. Each answers a different question:

ToolBest forWhat it forces
A3 Framing a problem and its countermeasures on one page Condensing to what is actually known and agreed
5-Why Driving a single causal chain past the first plausible answer Not stopping at "operator error"
8D Recurring or customer-facing failures needing formal closure Separating containment from permanent corrective action

The 8D distinction between containment and permanent corrective action is the one that breaks the recurrence loop. Containment protects the customer today; only permanent corrective action changes what the process produces tomorrow. Programs that treat them as the same step generate a lot of documentation and the same defects.

3. Work the MRB and NCR queue as evidence

I supported MRB and NCR investigations — material review board dispositions and nonconformance reports. That queue is the highest-quality defect data a plant has: every entry is a confirmed, documented failure with a part number and a disposition attached. Treated as a record-keeping obligation it produces nothing; treated as a dataset it tells you precisely which causes are recurring and what they cost. Working it that way cut rework and scrap by 13%.

4. Close causes out in the documents

Root causes traced back to the process definition were closed there rather than on the floor. I closed PFMEA action items cross-functionally with design, quality, and operations, and applied GD&T and DFM/DFA review to engineering drawings — because a defect caused by an ambiguous tolerance or an assembly sequence that cannot be performed reliably is a drawing problem, and no amount of operator training will remove it.

This is also where quality work stops being reactive. A PFMEA action item closed properly prevents the defect on the next program, not just this one, and NPI launches inherit the corrected process rather than repeating the discovery.

What generalizes

  • Recurrence is the signal. A defect that returns identifies a containment loop, and that is where the recoverable cost is concentrated.
  • Containment is not corrective action. Conflating them is the single most common reason quality programs run hard and change nothing.
  • The MRB/NCR queue is a dataset. It is already collected, already verified, and usually unmined.
  • "Operator error" is where 5-Why should get harder, not stop. It is a description of the last step, not a cause.
  • Close causes in the documents. A cause closed only on the floor comes back on the next program.
