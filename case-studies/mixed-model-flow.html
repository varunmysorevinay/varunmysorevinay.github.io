Fixed-position builds → mixed-model flow

A Lean transformation that moved heavy rail equipment off stationary assembly and onto balanced flow — coordinated across operations, quality, and planning.

30% Lead time reduction
VSM Kaizen · Gemba
3 Functions coordinated: operations, quality, planning

The situation

Heavy equipment is usually built where it sits. The machine occupies a bay, and people, parts, and tools come to it. Fixed-position assembly is the default for large products, and it is not wrong — but it hides an enormous amount of waste, because nothing about the layout forces the work to be balanced.

In a fixed-position build, a station that runs long does not visibly block anything. There is no line to stop. The delay simply disappears into the bay and reappears weeks later as a late delivery, and nobody can point to where it came from.

Fixed-position assembly does not remove the imbalance in a line. It removes your ability to see it.

What I did

1. Map the current state — at the Gemba

I ran value stream mapping across the build, grounded in Gemba observation rather than in what the routing said the process was. Those two are rarely the same document. A VSM built from ERP data describes the process as designed; a VSM built from watching describes the process as run, including the informal workarounds that are usually where the lead time actually goes.

The value stream map separates processing time from elapsed time — and in fixed-position work the gap between the two is typically the largest single opportunity on the map. Material waiting, information waiting, and queueing for shared resources dominate, not the work itself.

2. Convert to mixed-model flow

The core change was moving from fixed-position builds to mixed-model flow: different machine models moving through a shared sequence of balanced stations rather than each sitting in its own bay for the duration.

Mixed-model is the harder version of flow, and the necessary one in high-mix, low-volume work. It requires that stations be balanced against the mix rather than against a single product — station content has to work for every model that passes through it, not just the most common one. In exchange, it converts invisible bay-time into visible station-time, which is what makes imbalance addressable at all.

3. Drive the change through Kaizen

I facilitated Kaizen events to make the conversion, working the changes with the people who run the process rather than presenting them a new layout. This is not a courtesy. Operators know which sequence steps are genuinely order-dependent and which are merely habit, and that distinction determines what can actually be rebalanced.

4. Coordinate across functions

Flow conversion fails when it is treated as an operations project. I coordinated implementation across operations, quality, and planning — because moving to flow changes each of their jobs:

  • Operations gains station-based standard work in place of whole-machine ownership.
  • Quality has to move from end-of-build inspection to in-station verification, or it becomes the new bottleneck.
  • Planning has to release work to a takt rather than to bay availability, or the line starves and floods by turns.

Any one of those left unchanged is enough to pull the line back to fixed-position behaviour inside a quarter.

Results

MeasureOutcome
Lead timeReduced 30%
Assembly methodFixed-position builds → mixed-model flow
VisibilityImbalance surfaced at stations instead of hiding in bays
ScopeImplemented across operations, quality, and planning

A 30% lead time reduction is worth more than the number suggests. Lead time is what a customer experiences, what working capital is tied up in, and what forecasting error is proportional to. Cutting it improves quoted delivery, WIP, and schedule stability at the same time.

What generalizes

  • Map what is run, not what is documented. A VSM assembled from routings will confirm your assumptions and find nothing.
  • Elapsed time minus processing time is the opportunity. In fixed-position work that gap is usually most of the lead time.
  • Balance against the mix, not against a product. Mixed-model station content must hold for every model in the sequence.
  • Quality and planning have to move too. End-of-line inspection and bay-based release will both quietly undo a flow conversion.
