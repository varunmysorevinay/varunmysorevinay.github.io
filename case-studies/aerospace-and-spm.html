Aerospace components and special-purpose machines

Where the high-mix, low-volume discipline came from: aerospace component lines, and one-off machine builds for national aerospace and R&D programs.

73% Manufacturing efficiency improvement
15% Average project cost saving
25% Fewer late-stage engineering changes
12% Defect reduction under ISO 9001

Part one — Dynatech Tools and Devices

Manufacturing Engineer · Aerospace and precision tools supplier · Jun 2018 – Sep 2019

The situation

Aerospace component production carries two constraints at once: tight tolerances that cannot be negotiated, and an ISO 9001 quality system that means process changes have to be documented, not just made. Improvement work has to move within that, not around it.

What I did

I improved manufacturing efficiency 73% across aerospace component production lines through value stream mapping and time-motion studies, eliminating non-value-added content using DMAIC, SMED, and JIT.

SMED did most of the work, and the reason is structural. Precision component production runs in small batches with frequent changeovers, so setup is not overhead around the work — in high-mix production it is often a third or more of the total load. Converting internal setup (machine stopped) to external setup (machine running) attacks the largest block of lost time available without touching the cutting process itself, and without disturbing a validated machining operation.

In high-mix production, setup is not overhead around the work. It frequently is the work, measured in hours.

Alongside the flow work I standardized shop-floor work instructions and procedures, reducing operator variation and achieving a 12% defect reduction while maintaining ISO 9001 compliance. Reduced variation between operators is what makes any measured improvement real: without it, a good result is indistinguishable from a good shift.

Part two — SR Enterprises

Industrial Engineer · Special-purpose machines for Indian aerospace programs and national R&D organizations · Nov 2019 – Aug 2021

The situation

Special-purpose machines are the extreme case of high-mix, low-volume: a batch size of one, built to a specification that is often still being settled while fabrication is underway, for customers — aerospace programs and national R&D organizations — who accept the machine against formal criteria rather than simply receiving it.

There is no second unit to recover a mistake on. Every error is absorbed by the only build.

What I did

I managed special-purpose machine builds through workflow planning, risk mitigation, and customer sign-off, delivering a 15% average project cost saving. With a batch size of one, cost control is almost entirely a matter of front-loading: risks identified before fabrication are cheap, and the same risks discovered during assembly are not.

I redesigned build flows using VSM, Kaizen, and 5S, reducing late-stage engineering changes 25% and smoothing handoffs between fabrication and assembly. Late-stage engineering change is the characteristic failure of one-off builds and the most expensive one — the change arrives after the affected work is already done. Cutting it by a quarter was worth more than any equivalent gain in touch time.

I applied PFMEA and root-cause analysis to eliminate recurring sources of process variation, improving first-pass reliability on aerospace-grade components — and delivered manufacturing readiness for R&D customers: equipment validation, first-article verification, and the process documentation required for customer acceptance.

That last item is a discipline of its own. For these customers the machine is not delivered when it works; it is delivered when it has been demonstrated to work, against agreed criteria, with evidence. Building the documentation trail alongside the machine rather than after it is the difference between a sign-off and a dispute.

What this period established

  • High-mix, low-volume as the default. Batch sizes of one to a few dozen, where standard mass-production assumptions simply do not apply — the environment I have worked in ever since.
  • Setup reduction as a first move. When changeovers are frequent, SMED is usually the largest available gain and among the least disruptive.
  • Front-loading risk. In low-volume work the cost of an error scales with how late it is found, which makes planning and PFMEA the highest-leverage hours in the project.
  • Documentation as part of the deliverable. Regulated and R&D customers accept evidence, not assurances.
