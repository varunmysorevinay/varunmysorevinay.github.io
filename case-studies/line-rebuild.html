Harsco Rail · Manufacturing Engineer

Rebuilding a remanufacturing line from scratch

Work that had gone to competitors, brought back by rebuilding the line that lost it — layout, standard work, capacity model, and cost structure.

63% → 84% Line efficiency, no headcount added
$195,000 Cost saving delivered
~$30M Business targeted for recovery

The situation

Harsco Rail builds and maintains railway maintenance and track construction equipment. Remanufacturing — taking machines back in, stripping them, and rebuilding them to specification — is high-mix, low-volume work by nature: no two incoming machines are in the same condition, and the work content varies with what you find when you open them up.

That variability had been allowed to become the excuse. The line ran at 63% efficiency, and a substantial volume of remanufacturing business — on the order of $30M — had already been lost to competitors who could quote it cheaper and deliver it faster.

A line does not lose work because its product is complicated. It loses work because nobody has measured where the time actually goes.

What I did

I rebuilt the line from scratch rather than optimizing the existing one — layout design, SOPs, capacity planning, and cost reduction analysis as one connected program.

1. Measure before touching anything

The first work was time studies across the build, element by element. Not to set labor standards — to find out where the content actually was, and how much of it was value-added. In remanufacturing, a large share of elapsed time typically turns out to be waiting, walking, searching, and re-handling rather than work on the machine.

Timing elements individually also exposes which ones are variable versus merely long. Those need completely different responses: a long stable element is a candidate for method improvement, while a highly variable one is usually a queue or a material problem in disguise.

2. Find the constraint

With element times in hand, bottleneck analysis identified which station was actually governing output. This matters more than it sounds. Improvement effort spent anywhere other than the constraint produces no additional throughput at all — it just creates more inventory in front of the same bottleneck.

3. Redesign the layout around the flow

I redesigned the physical layout in Solid Edge, positioning stations and material presentation around the sequence of work rather than around where equipment historically sat. Alongside the layout I designed custom tooling, jigs, and fixtures to take content out of the constraint stations and to make setups repeatable instead of dependent on individual technique.

4. Lock it in with standard work

A rebalanced line reverts within weeks unless the new method is the documented method. I standardized SOPs and designed ergonomic workstation layouts so the improved sequence was also the easiest sequence to follow — and so that the capacity model reflected what the line would actually do, not what it did on a good day.

5. Model the capacity and the cost

Capacity planning and cost reduction analysis closed the loop, turning the rebuilt line into quotable numbers — which is what recovering lost business actually requires. A faster line that cannot be costed credibly does not win work back.

Results

The efficiency gain came with no headcount increase. That distinction is the whole point: 21 points of efficiency bought by adding people is a cost increase wearing a productivity label. Bought by removing non-value-added content and rebalancing the work, it is margin.

What generalizes

  • High variability is a finding, not an obstacle. Remanufacturing content genuinely varies — but that argues for flexible standard work and buffered constraints, not for abandoning measurement.
  • Improve the constraint or improve nothing. Effort spent off the bottleneck converts directly into WIP.
  • Layout is a lever, not a given. Most lines are laid out around history. Re-laying them out around the actual sequence of work is often the cheapest large gain available.
  • Standard work is what makes it permanent. Without it, the line drifts back to where it started and the savings evaporate quietly.
